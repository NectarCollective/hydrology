Streamflow & Floods

A river is a moving record of everything that happened upstream.

Streamflow (or discharge) is how much water passes a point in a river each second. In the US it's measured in cubic feet per second, or cfs. One cfs is about 7.5 gallons every second.

How it's measured

Thousands of stream gauges track the water level, called stage, every few minutes. Hydrologists measure the flow by hand at different levels, then build a rating curve that turns stage into flow. That's how a gauge reports live discharge.

Baseflow vs. stormflow

BaseflowThe steady water that keeps rivers running between storms, mostly seeping in from groundwater.
StormflowThe quick surge after rain or snowmelt, from runoff and shallow soil water.

Reading a hydrograph

A hydrograph is a graph of flow over time.

  1. Rising limb. Rain reaches the river and flow climbs.
  2. Peak. The highest flow, often hours or days after the heaviest rain. That delay is the lag time.
  3. Falling limb. Flow drops back toward baseflow as the watershed drains.

Small, steep, or paved watersheds have short lag times and sharp peaks. Big, flat, or forested ones respond slower and gentler.

What causes floods

  1. Heavy or long rain that falls faster than the ground can absorb it.
  2. Rain on snow, which adds snowmelt to the rain.
  3. Saturated or frozen ground, so nothing soaks in.
  4. Pavement and storm drains, which rush water into streams.
  5. Flash floods come from intense rain over small, steep areas and can rise in minutes.

Why it matters

Streamflow drives flood warnings, tells cities and farms how much water they'll have, and shows how healthy a river is for fish.
